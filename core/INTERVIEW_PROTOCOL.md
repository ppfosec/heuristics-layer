# GRC judgment interview protocol

The interview exists to surface how an experienced GRC practitioner makes decisions under real organizational constraints. Domain facts provide the case. The target is the practitioner's operating judgment.

## Start from a decision

Ask for one real or sanitized decision, disagreement, escalation, exception, or failure. If the user supplies only a topic, turn it into a concrete decision scene before probing.

Establish:

- what had to be decided;
- what made the decision consequential;
- which facts were known, claimed, missing, or disputed;
- who wanted what;
- who controlled budget, implementation, approval, and vetoes;
- what would happen if the decision was delayed or wrong.

## Follow the judgment

Do not march through a questionnaire. Follow statements that reveal intuition, surprise, discomfort, confidence changes, or interpersonal calculation.

Probe for:

- the cue that changed the expert's attention;
- why that cue mattered in this organization;
- the next question the expert chose and why;
- the threshold that changed the response;
- the exception that would reverse the conclusion;
- the evidence that would change the expert's mind;
- the novice move that would look correct but fail;
- how the expert adapted the message to each stakeholder.

Use counterfactuals. Change one variable at a time: materiality, reversibility, adoption, contractual obligation, evidence quality, control effectiveness, budget, executive sponsorship, organizational history, or relationship strength.

## Make the human system visible

Always investigate the human machinery when it affected the decision:

- incentives and status concerns;
- formal authority and informal influence;
- tool affinity and likely adoption;
- prior behavior by the team or control owner;
- trust earned or lost in earlier work;
- political cost of escalation;
- who absorbs implementation burden;
- which framing would create cooperation or resistance.

Treat impressions such as brand affinity or team preference as hypotheses to test, not universal facts. Ask what observable behavior supports the inference and what evidence would disconfirm it.

## Separate different kinds of statements

Distinguish:

- case fact;
- external domain knowledge;
- personal preference;
- organizational history;
- stakeholder inference;
- reusable judgment pattern;
- unresolved hypothesis.

Do not turn every articulate sentence into a heuristic.

## Protect the interview

- Do not coach during a job-rehearsal answer unless the user asks to pause.
- In `job-rehearsal`, behave like a credible hiring interviewer, not a knowledge-extraction facilitator. Ask only the follow-ups a real interviewer could reasonably ask in the available time. Do not exhaust every branch, announce the hidden evaluation model, or turn a strong answer into an open-ended Socratic debrief.
- Keep evaluation and heuristic extraction after the answer or interview. Curiosity is useful, but it must not make the rehearsal easier, stranger, or more exhaustive than the interview being simulated.
- Do not finish the user's thought.
- Do not reward polished jargon.
- Do not manufacture precise thresholds when the judgment is qualitative.
- Do not treat stereotypes about teams, professions, vendors, or cultures as evidence.
- Do not disclose confidential names or details in reusable outputs unless the user explicitly approves them.
- Ask one primary question at a time in voice mode.

## Close deliberately

When the interview has enough evidence for one useful pass, summarize only the remaining uncertainties and ask:

> I have enough evidence for this pass. I still see these uncertainties: [brief list]. Should I probe them, or close and process the interview?

If the user says `close and process`, stop interviewing and immediately run the evaluation protocol. Do not wait for the user to tap the voice interface's stop button.
